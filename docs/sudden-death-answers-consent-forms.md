## 1. Next-of-kin / executor authorization

**Purpose:** Establishes who may authorize sample collection and records release when the decedent cannot consent.

Draft language:

> I, ______________________ ("Authorizing Party"), certify that I am the next of kin, legally appointed executor, or holder of healthcare power of attorney (documentation attached) for ______________________ ("Decedent"), who died on ____________.
>
> I authorize TabulaMedica and its designated laboratory, imaging, and toxicology partners to:
> 1. Collect buccal and dried-blood-spot samples, and/or use previously banked specimens, from the Decedent for genetic testing.
> 2. Perform postmortem CT / CT angiography imaging.
> 3. Perform toxicology testing on collected specimens.
> 4. Access relevant clinical records held in TabulaMedica PHR or obtained from other providers, for the purpose of records review by a cardiologist or pathologist.
>
> I understand this evaluation is **not a legal autopsy** and does not replace a medical examiner's or coroner's determination when one has jurisdiction. Results are provided to the certifying physician or medical examiner and to the family; they do not themselves certify a legal cause of death.

**Open items for counsel:** state-by-state next-of-kin hierarchy (spouse > adult child > parent > sibling, varies by state), executor documentation requirements, minor-decedent parental consent variant.

## 2. Medical examiner / coroner coordination consent

**Purpose:** Used when the ME/coroner has declined jurisdiction or released the body without autopsy, to document that release status before collection proceeds.

Draft language:

> The undersigned confirms that the Office of the Medical Examiner / Coroner for ______________________ County has [ ] declined jurisdiction over this death, or [ ] released the body without performing an autopsy, as evidenced by: ______________________ (release form, verbal confirmation with name/date/time logged, or case number).
>
> TabulaMedica's non-invasive evaluation may proceed. If the ME/coroner's office later asserts jurisdiction, all collected specimens and results will be made available to that office on request.

**Open items for counsel:** verbal-confirmation logging standard, what happens if ME asserts jurisdiction after collection has started, retention obligations for specimens in that scenario.

## 3. Honest resolution-rate disclosure

**Purpose:** The single most important disclosure — must be read and separately initialed, not buried in boilerplate.

Draft language (must appear in plain, large-type, separately initialed):

> **What this evaluation can and cannot tell you.** Combining postmortem CT/CT angiography, needle-only toxicology, clinical records review, and genetic testing, most families receive a probable cause of death. A definite genetic cause is identified in approximately **13-30%** of cases that were truly unexplained going in. This evaluation **cannot** reliably detect pulmonary embolism without contrast administration, myocarditis, early-stage heart attack, or some inherited heart-rhythm disorders (channelopathies) — a traditional autopsy remains the gold standard for these. If you want the highest-certainty answer regardless of cost or timeline, ask about referral to a forensic pathologist for a full autopsy instead of, or in addition to, this service.
>
> Initials: ______

**Open items for counsel:** confirm the 13-30% figure is sourced and dated at time of use (cite the studies in the launch brief's sources section), state-specific informed-consent requirements for genetic testing, whether a "cooling off" period or second-signature requirement applies.

## 4. Genetic testing / GINA disclosure

Draft language:

> Genetic results may be relevant to your own and other relatives' health and insurance. Federal law (GINA) prohibits health insurers and most employers from using this information against you, but **does not** cover life, disability, or long-term-care insurance. Consider the timing of this testing relative to any pending insurance applications, and ask your genetic counselor if you have questions.
